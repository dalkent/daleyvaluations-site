Methodology

The valuation framework behind every signal on this site. Three independent models, one blended target, applied weekly to 114 FTSE stocks.

Most stock commentary is opinion dressed up as analysis. The work here goes the other way. Every signal you see on this site comes from a systematic, data-driven valuation framework I have built and refined over the last decade. The methodology runs the same way on every stock, every week, with the inputs and assumptions written down so you can argue with them.

This page explains how the framework works, why it is built that way, and where it falls short. If you are going to trust the signals on this site, you should know exactly what is producing them.

The three models

Each FTSE stock is valued using up to three independent methods. Three is the right number. One model is fragile. Two cannot resolve a disagreement. Three lets you spot when one is misbehaving and gives you a way to weight the rest.

1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

Project the company's free cash flow forward five years, add a terminal value for everything beyond that, and discount the whole stream back to today using a cost of capital appropriate to the stock's risk. This is the textbook intrinsic-value model and the most theoretically pure of the three.

It is also the most sensitive. A 1% move in the discount rate can swing a DCF target by 20-30%. Small changes in the growth assumption produce large changes in the answer. So I treat the DCF output as a directional signal, not a precise number.

For banks, insurers and other financial firms, the DCF is unreliable because operating cash flow is dominated by lending and deposits rather than business cash generation. For these stocks the DCF is skipped entirely and a sector-specific model takes its place.

2. Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

Value the stock as the present value of every future dividend it will ever pay. The classic Gordon Growth formulation: next year's expected dividend divided by the gap between the discount rate and the long-run dividend growth rate.

The DDM is best for mature, dividend-paying companies with stable payout policies. It produces no answer at all for stocks that pay nothing, and it produces a misleading answer for stocks whose dividend is variable or commodity-linked. Mining companies, in particular, break this model and are excluded from the DDM blend.

3. Earnings Power Value (EPV)

Forward earnings per share multiplied by a normalised price-to-earnings multiple. The third model deliberately ignores future growth, on the principle that if a business is genuinely worth what it earns today, anything more is gravy. The EPV typically produces the most conservative of the three numbers and acts as a floor.

The P/E used is capped at 22.5x to stop a market price already inflated by growth expectations from feeding back into the model and producing a circular result.

Sub-sector classification within financials

Book-value-anchored methods work well for capital-heavy traditional financials where balance sheet equity is a meaningful proxy for what the business owns. They break for asset-light, high-ROE specialists where the value sits in the brand, the underwriting discipline or the distribution model rather than the balance sheet. To deal with this, every financial-sector stock is classified into one of three sub-sectors and the book-anchored and earnings-anchored methods are blended in different proportions:

The earnings-anchored secondary uses normalised earnings per share, computed as a three-year average of underlying EPS with mark-to-market and one-off items excluded. If the normalisation breaks (negative or distorted recent EPS) the model falls back to the book method only and the ticker is flagged. The justified P/E used in the secondary is taken from a sub-sector lookup table published in the assumptions section below.

The blend

Once each applicable model has produced a number, the framework reduces them to a single Target Price using two steps.

Step one is outlier removal. Any model value that lands more than three times above or below the median of the others is excluded. This catches the case where one model has clearly broken: a DCF coming in at 25p for a 500p stock, or an EPV running away because of a tech-sector P/E. The threshold is symmetric, so a model that is too low gets removed as readily as one that is too high.

Step two is an equal-weight average. All surviving model values are given the same weight. There is no attempt to weight by historical accuracy or analyst confidence. The models are treated as three independent estimates of the same underlying number, and their disagreement is itself information.

The blended Target Price is then divided by the current market price to produce the Daley Value Ratio (DVR). That ratio drives the signal.

Five signals, set by where the DVR falls:

The thresholds put a 10% band of "close enough" around the current price. Inside that band, the model is not telling you anything actionable. Outside it, the size of the gap is itself the signal: a Strong Buy is more than just a Buy, and the model is committing to that view.

Where the model can see, and where it can't

I am open about what this framework does and does not do. A signal generated here is a starting point for research, not the end of it.

What the model sees: the company's reported earnings, dividends, cash flows, balance sheet, sector classification, and current market price. It values each stock against its own fundamentals, on the same set of rules every other stock in the universe is valued against. That consistency is the point.

What the model does not see: the quality of the management team, the durability of the competitive moat, the regulatory or geopolitical risks specific to the business, the credibility of forward guidance, or anything that requires judgement rather than calculation. These matter enormously, and I write about them in the deep dives. But they don't appear in the numbers on this page.

Where the model is least reliable:

  • Mining and commodity traders (Glencore, Anglo American, Rio Tinto). Free cash flow is cyclical, dividends are variable, and reported earnings can swing on a single year of commodity prices. Treat signals here as low confidence and lean on analyst consensus and sector multiples instead.
  • Pre-profit growth stocks. If there are no earnings and no dividend, two of the three models go silent and the EPV is doing all the work. The framework was not built for this and the signal will reflect that.
  • Companies in transition. Spin-offs, large acquisitions, and major restructurings all break the assumption that historical financials predict future ones. The model will value the past; the price is reacting to the future.
  • Recent demergers and unusual capital structures. Companies that have just demerged, listed, or gone through a major recapitalisation may not have three clean years of underlying earnings to feed the normalisation rule. Where this happens the framework falls back to a shorter base or, if that is not reliable either, to the book method only. The signal is still produced but its confidence is lower, and the article on each name will say so explicitly.

Not financial advice

The signals on this site are a systematic, data-driven starting point for investment research. They do not account for management quality, competitive position, regulatory risk, or any of the qualitative judgements that separate a working thesis from a number on a screen. Always do your own research before acting on any signal you see here.
