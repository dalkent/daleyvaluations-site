Methodology

The rules behind every signal on this site. The same rules run on all 102 FTSE stocks, and every number is written down below.

Most stock commentary is opinion dressed up as analysis. The work here goes the other way. Every signal comes from a systematic valuation framework that builds on twenty years of valuation work. The current rules are written down below, with dates, so you can check them and argue with them.

The framework is a deliberately strict value lens. It asks what a business is worth on its current cash, dividends and earnings, with modest growth. Fast-growing, high-return companies will often look expensive through it. That is a choice, not an accident, and the ticker pages show what growth today's price assumes so you can see the gap.

From data to signal

  1. Route. Each stock goes down one of two paths: financials (banks, insurers, asset managers and other financial firms) or everything else.
  2. Value. Up to three models each produce a fair value per share.
  3. Blend. The values are combined with weights fixed in advance for that type of business.
  4. Compare. The blended target divided by today's price gives the Daley Value Ratio (DVR).
  5. Signal. The DVR sets the signal. Tags flag signals that rest on thin evidence.

The discount rate

Every model discounts future cash at a rate set by the stock's risk: the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium. For financial firms the same rate is called the cost of equity (Ke), because a bank's borrowing is part of its business, not a financing choice.

Risk-free rate, UK (10-year gilt)5.4%
Risk-free rate, US (10-year Treasury)4.9%
Equity risk premium5%
Beta range0.5 to 2.5. A missing beta is treated as 1
Beta floor, Basic Materials and Energy1.1

A UK stock with a beta of 1.0 is discounted at 10.4%. The higher floor for basic materials and energy exists because measured betas for oil and mining companies often sit near zero as commodity prices drift away from the stock market. Without it, a miner would get a lower discount rate than a utility.

The risk-free rates are set to the market 10-year yield at each quarterly review, using the spot rate. A move of 0.5 percentage points or more between reviews triggers an early update. The equity risk premium is never adjusted to offset a move in rates: that would be working back from the answer.

The three models for non-financial companies

1. Discounted cash flow (DCF)

Free cash flow to shareholders, averaged over up to three years, grown at 5% a year for five years and 2.5% a year after that, discounted back to today.

  • Cash flow to shareholders. Operating cash flow less capital spending, less interest paid, plus interest received. The discount rate is a cost of equity, so the cash flow must be what is left for shareholders.
  • Leases. For UK companies, lease repayments due in the next year are deducted. Under current accounting rules they sit outside operating cash flow, which would otherwise flatter retailers, airlines and other lease-heavy businesses.
  • A bad year. If any of the three years is negative, the cash flow average is not used. Instead the model takes trailing earnings times a sector conversion ratio:
    SectorCash flow as a share of earnings
    Utilities85%
    Consumer Defensive80%
    Industrials75%
    Energy70%
    Basic Materials65%
    Other sectors have no fallback, so no DCF. Basic Materials never uses the fallback: mining earnings at a cycle low say nothing useful about long-run cash flow.

The DCF is the most sensitive of the three. One percentage point on the discount rate can move it by 20 to 30%. Treat it as directional.

2. Dividend discount model (DDM)

Next year's dividend divided by the gap between the discount rate and 2.5% long-run dividend growth. No dividend, no value.

The dividend comes from the declared annual rate where available, otherwise from the dividend yield or the trailing twelve months of payments. Where two sources disagree by more than 25%, the lower is used.

The DDM is not used for Basic Materials or Energy. Mining payouts follow commodity prices, and oil majors return much of their cash through buybacks, so a perpetuity on the cash dividend misstates both.

3. Earnings power value (EPV)

Earnings per share (forward if available, otherwise trailing) times 15, the long-run market average multiple.

The earnings check. EPV is dropped, not estimated, when trailing earnings are zero or negative, or when forward earnings are less than 0.5x or more than 2x trailing. In both cases today's earnings are not a reliable guide to what the business earns in a normal year. Where the ratio sits within 10% of either limit, the stock is tagged "Earnings check borderline", because a small change in forecasts could move it across the line.

How the models are blended

Each UK company is assigned to one of five business types, and each type has fixed weights:

Business typeDCFDDMEPVDefault for
Mature compounder50%20%30%Consumer Defensive, Healthcare, Technology
Mature cyclical20%20%60%Consumer Cyclical, Industrials
Yield anchor25%50%25%Utilities, Real Estate, Communication Services
Capex cyclical50%25%25%Energy, Basic Materials
Restructuring0%50%50%Assigned per stock

A company can be assigned a different type from its sector default where its business warrants it. Weights are set in advance by business type because an equal-weight blend with outliers removed jumped about: in May 2026 one stock's target halved on a 4% move in a single input.

When a model is missing. If the DCF or EPV has no value, its weight moves to the other one. If the DDM is missing, the DCF and EPV share the whole blend. The DDM never picks up extra weight, so a dividend can never take over a valuation by default.

Dividend-led companies. For the yield-anchor type only, the DCF is capped at 2x the higher of the DDM and EPV, so that a long cash flow projection cannot outweigh the dividend that actually sets value for these businesses.

Everything else. Unclassified and international stocks use an equal-weight average, with each model held within 3x of the median of the three. Values are held, not removed, so the target moves smoothly as inputs move.

Last resort. If no model produces a value, the target is the analyst consensus and the signal is tagged "Consensus".

Financial companies

Cash flow models do not work for banks and insurers, because their operating cash flow is mostly customer money moving through the balance sheet. Banks, insurers and asset managers use a blend of up to three parts, all discounted at Ke:

  • Book value (banks only): book value times a justified price-to-book of 1 + (ROE − Ke) ÷ (Ke − 2.5%), held between 0.3x and 3x. A bank that earns more than its cost of equity is worth more than its book.
  • Dividends: the cash dividend, grown at 2.5%. Buybacks are excluded. The profit that funds them is already counted in the other two parts, and treating a capital release as a permanent payout made almost every bank look like a Strong Buy.
  • Earnings: earnings per share divided by Ke, with the same earnings check as EPV.

Banks40%30%30%
General InsuranceNot used50%50%
Life InsuranceNot used70%30%
Asset ManagementNot used50%50%

Insurers and asset managers get no book value part. Insurance accounting leaves future profit on existing policies out of equity, so book understates what an insurer owns, and an asset manager's value is not on its balance sheet at all.

HSBC and Standard Chartered carry an extra 1% on Ke for emerging-market risk.

  • Investment trusts and other closed-end vehicles: net asset value per share less a 5% discount.
  • Private equity and alternatives: book value times 1.1.
  • Exchanges and capital markets: EBITDA times 16, less net debt, per share.
  • Other financial services: an equal-weight average of the dividend model, earnings divided by Ke, and a residual income value.

III.L and PSH.L are not published. The flat 5% discount misleads for both: Pershing Square has traded around 40% below its net asset value for years, and 3i usually trades near or above it. They will return when the method uses each trust's own long-run discount.

Currency

Many UK-listed companies report in dollars or euros. Earnings, cash flows, book values and dividends are converted from the reporting currency to sterling before any model runs, so a dollar of Shell's earnings is never treated as a pound.

A signal is a reason to look closer, not a decision. Three tags show where the evidence is thin:

  • 1 model: the target rests on a single model, so nothing cross-checks it.
  • Consensus: no model produced a value and the target is the analyst consensus.
  • Caution (tracker table): the signal could flip on a small change. The earnings check is borderline, the forward view disagrees, or a Strong Buy depends on one model alone.
  • Ticker pages list every check, including where removing one model alone would move a Strong Sell out of its band. For a Strong Sell this usually means a small dividend, which is the strict value lens at work rather than a fragile signal.

The forward view. Where the earnings check has dropped a stock's earnings model, the ticker page also shows what the same model gives if consensus earnings forecasts arrive. It is a recovery case, shown for context. It never changes the signal.

What the price assumes. For companies with a DCF, the ticker page shows the five-year cash flow growth that today's price implies, against the model's 5%. A Strong Sell on a company priced for 30% a year growth tells you about the expectations, not necessarily that the business is poor.

A valuation older than 7 days is not published.

Where the model is weakest

  • Growth companies. Flat 5% growth and a 15x multiple make high-return growers look expensive. Read their signals alongside what the price assumes.
  • Miners and commodity traders. Cash flow swings, the dividend model is off, and earnings move with commodity prices. Many sit on one model. Low confidence; use sector multiples and consensus alongside.
  • Utilities. Heavy investment often pushes them onto the earnings-based cash flow fallback. The dividend model is the most reliable part.
  • Property companies. They are valued as dividend-led businesses. A net asset value method would suit them better.
  • Depleting assets. An oil field does not grow at 2.5% for ever, and decommissioning costs are not treated as debt.
  • Insurers. Underwriting quality and embedded value are not in the model.
  • Data. Everything comes from public market data, which sometimes changes or disappears without notice.
  • Judgement. The model knows nothing about management, competitive position, regulation or politics. The deep dives on Substack cover those.
